Reading a Menu

Lesson 0010 · ~15 minutes · Reading track · Katakana in the wild

You've drilled 92 characters. This is the lesson where they start paying for themselves.

It's also the last reading lesson for a while. Character practice belongs in the trainer from here — it's better at it than any lesson is, and you were right to say so. But there are three things a one-character drill structurally cannot teach you, and all three are on every menu in Japan. That's today.

1. Katakana is a cheat code

Katakana marks a word as foreign. On a menu that nearly always means English. So the job isn't translation — it's this:

Sound it out, then listen to yourself. Don't try to recognise the word by shape. Say the syllables out loud, badly, in a Japanese accent, and the English word usually falls out of your own mouth.

You've already done this once without noticing. Back in Lesson 2 you read ビール off a sign: bi-i-ru → biiru → beer. Same move, every time.

2. The three marks that aren't in your drill

ー — the long bar

A plain horizontal bar means hold the vowel you just said. It's not a character and it has no sound of its own.

コーヒー = ko — o — hi — i = koohii = coffee.

Skip the bars and you get kohi, which is nothing. The bars are most of the word.

ッ — the small tsu

A ツ written small and low. It isn't a sound — it's a beat of silence that doubles the next consonant.

チケット = chi — ke — (stop) — to = chiketto = ticket.

Size is the whole signal. ツ big is tsu, a real syllable. ッ small is a pause. Same for ヤユヨ vs ャュョ below. If a character looks undersized and sits low, it is not itself.

ャ ュ ョ — the small ya/yu/yo

These glue onto the character before them and kill its vowel, making one syllable out of two characters.

BigSmall
ニ + ユ = ni-yu, two beatsニ + ュ = nyu, one beat
シ + ヨ = shi-yoシ + ョ = sho

メニュー = me — nyu — u = menyuu = menu.

4. When the sound-out only gets you halfway

Japanese clips long loanwords, usually to four syllables. If your sound-out produces the first half of an English word and then stops, you've probably already got it.

5. A real drink menu

Everything below uses only what's above. 円 is the kanji for en — the yen sign you learned in Lesson 2. You'll see it on every price in the country.

ホットコーヒー４００円
アイスコーヒー４５０円
オレンジジュース５００円
ビール６００円
ケーキ７００円

ホット (hotto) and アイス (aisu) sit in front of every drink on every café menu in Japan. Note the small ッ doing its job in ホット.

Now close the loop. You can read it, so order it — with the pattern from Lesson 1:
アイスコーヒーを ください。
Read the menu, point, say the word, add を ください. That is a complete transaction and you have every piece of it.
One more you'll meet: small ァ ィ ゥ ェ ォ exist too, for sounds Japanese doesn't have — ティー (tii, tea), フォーク (fooku, fork), ウェブ (webu, web). Same rule as the small ya/yu/yo: glue it to the character before.

7. Real-world rep (this week)

Katakana is not only in Japan. Pull up the menu of any ramen or sushi place near you, or search Japanese convenience store in image search, and read the signs out loud. You'll be slow at first — that's fine, slow-and-correct is the stage before fast. Every word you decode is a word you won't have to decode on the trip.
