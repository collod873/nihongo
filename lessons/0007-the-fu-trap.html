The fu Trap

Lesson 0007 · ~15 minutes · Reading track · Katakana only

Your clean Full 92 came back 67 correct, 49 solid. That's the first honest baseline you've had — 92 unique kana, no repeats, no skips. And buried in it is a finding that changes what we drill.

1. First, the win

ク / ケ was your worst leech last time — swapped in both directions, five misses. This run: ケ solid, ク correct. Gone. So were ハ/ホ, ル/リ, ト/よ and お/あ.

Why that happened is the useful part. The last run had a bug that accidentally served ク and ケ four or five times each in one session. That's the only pair that got hammered like that, and it's the pair that resolved. Concentrated contrast in a single sitting beats the same reps spread over weeks. That's what this lesson is built to do.

2. You are not guessing — that's the problem

14 of your 25 misses came in under 1.8 seconds. Fast and wrong. You weren't stuck; you retrieved an answer confidently and it was the wrong one. Only five were genuine blanks.

This matters because the two failures need opposite fixes. A blank gets better with exposure. Confident-wrong gets worse with exposure — every rep rehearses the wrong link. What breaks it is a feature you can consciously check in the moment, before the fast answer fires.

3. The real finding: it's not pairs, it's a hub

Look at what you typed rather than what you were shown:

Three different characters all came back fu — and フ itself you read as su. So fu isn't one half of a pair you're flipping. It's your default answer for anything hook-shaped, and the character it belongs to isn't nailed down either.

Why this changes the drill: practising ヌ against ス would fix nothing here — ヲ and ワ would still route to fu. A four-way collapse needs all four on screen at once, each with its own distinguishing mark. That's this lesson.

4. The hook family, built from one shape

Here's the trick that makes this stick: every character in the family is フ plus exactly one thing. Learn the bare shape, then learn what each addition means. You stop pattern-matching whole characters and start reading one feature.

KanaSound= フ plus…
フfunothing. One bend, no extras. This is the base.
ラraa short stroke on top, stopping early
ヲwoa full-width bar across the top
ワwaa left vertical — the shoulder closes into a box
ウuワ + a hat on top
スsua leg kicking down-left from the middle
ヌnuス, but the leg crosses the bend
クkuthe top is a short tick, not a full bar
ケkeク + a stroke cutting across the diagonal

Three of these resolve on a question you can ask in under a second:

Top stroke? None → フ. Short → ラ. Full width → ヲ.
Left side closed? Yes → ワ (and with a hat, ウ).
Got a leg? Meets the bend → ス. Crosses it → ヌ.

Spend your effort proportionally. フ, ス, ク, ラ and ワ are everywhere in loanwords — フランス (France), クラス (class), スワップ. ヲ is the rarest character in Japanese: the particle is written を in hiragana, so you may go a whole trip without seeing ヲ once. Know it, don't agonise over it.

5. Drill it now, hard, in one sitting

Nine characters, 27 cards, all from this family. Misses show the discriminating feature and queue the character you confused it with. This is the concentrated contrast that fixed ク/ケ by accident — now on purpose.

Reminder: ヲ is typed wo. Under 1.8 seconds counts as solid — anything slower is reconstruction and stays in rotation.

6. What's next

The other hub is ne: チ and ナ in katakana, れ and ぬ in hiragana all came back as ne. It crosses both scripts, which is unusual, and it's Lesson 8.

Your hiragana needs no lessons — accuracy is fine (8 misses vs katakana's 17). What it lacks is speed: 10 of your 18 slow-but-correct answers were hiragana. That's trainer reps, not teaching.
