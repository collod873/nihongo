Greetings That Make Locals Light Up

Lesson 0003 · ~13 minutes · Speaking-first · Builds on: sumimasen, kudasai, numbers

A traveler who greets people properly gets warmer service, better recommendations, and real smiles. Japanese greetings are tied to the time of day and the situation — today you learn the four you'll use constantly, plus the thank-you that does heavy lifting everywhere.

1. The time-of-day trio

おはようございます ohayou gozaimasu "Good morning" (until ~10–11am)
こんにちは konnichiwa "Hello / good day" (late morning until dusk)
こんばんは konbanwa "Good evening" (after dark)
Why three words for hello? Japanese doesn't have an all-day "hi" — the greeting itself tells people you know what time it is. Using konnichiwa at 8am marks you as a beginner; ohayou gozaimasu at 8am marks you as someone who gets it. Easy win.

2. Thank you — and turning it up

ありがとうございます arigatou gozaimasu "Thank you" (polite — your default)

Plain arigatou is casual — fine with friends, a bit flat with staff. Adding gozaimasu makes it properly polite; that's your default as a traveler. Notice the pattern: it's the same polish that upgraded ohayou above. When someone really goes out of their way, lead with a hot sumimasen (thanks-for-the-trouble, from Lesson 1) and follow with arigatou gozaimasu — very natural, very Japanese.

The leaving-a-shop combo: as you exit any restaurant or shop, a nod plus arigatou gozaimasu — staff will beam. You'll hear them shout arigatou gozaimashita (the past-tense version) back at you; no need to produce that one yet, just recognize it.

3. Say them like a local

Two pronunciation notes, both about things romaji hides:

The は trick: こんにちは and こんばんは end in the kana は, which normally reads ha — but here it's pronounced wa. That's because it's the grammar particle wa (a fossilized "as for this day…"). Same particle you met in kore wa ikura desu ka. Kana probe incoming on this one.

The vanishing u: gozaimasu is said go-za-i-mas — the final u nearly disappears. Same with desu → "des." Listen:

Now drill out loud: morning voice — ohayou gozaimasu. Afternoon — konnichiwa. Night — konbanwa. Gratitude — arigatou gozaimasu. Five reps each, even beats.

5. Real-world rep (this week)

Greet your day in Japanese: first coffee — ohayou gozaimasu. Lunch — konnichiwa. Lights out — konbanwa. Anchoring each greeting to the actual time of day is exactly how they're stored in a native speaker's head.
